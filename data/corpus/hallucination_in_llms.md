# Hallucination in Large Language Models

In the context of large language models, hallucination refers to generated content that is fluent and confident-sounding but factually incorrect, unsupported by any source, or internally inconsistent with the prompt. The term is borrowed loosely from psychology, but in machine learning it describes a specific failure mode: the model is not malfunctioning in any obvious way, and the output usually reads as perfectly natural language — the problem is purely that the claims it contains are not true or not grounded in real evidence.

Researchers generally split hallucinations into two broad categories. Intrinsic hallucinations directly contradict the source material the model was given (for example, a summary that states the opposite of what the source document says). Extrinsic hallucinations introduce information that cannot be verified from the source at all — not necessarily false, but simply invented or added from the model's general training knowledge rather than the provided context. In a retrieval-augmented system, extrinsic hallucination is the more common and more dangerous failure, since a model may blend real retrieved facts with plausible-sounding but unretrieved details, making the fabricated parts hard to spot without checking each claim against the actual evidence.

The causes of hallucination are still an active research area, but several contributing factors are well documented. Language models are trained to predict plausible next tokens, not to verify truth, so a fluent continuation is optimized for over a correct one whenever the two diverge. Training data itself contains errors, contradictions, and outdated information, which models can absorb as if it were established fact. Long or ambiguous prompts increase the chance that a model loses track of exactly what it was given versus what it "remembers," and models are also known to exhibit sycophancy — generating an answer that matches what the question seems to expect, even if that answer is not well supported.

Mitigation strategies broadly fall into two groups. The first works before or during generation: grounding the model in retrieved evidence (retrieval-augmented generation), using more constrained decoding, or prompting the model explicitly to say "I don't know" when it lacks support. The second works after generation: verifying each generated claim against evidence using entailment classifiers, semantic similarity scores, or a second "fact-checking" model pass, and then deciding whether to show, flag, or block the output based on that verification. A hallucination-detection framework that blocks or partially suppresses a response when verification fails — rather than only flagging it after the fact — sits in this second category, and is generally considered a stronger safeguard for high-stakes or offline deployments where a human reviewer may not be available to catch every unflagged error.
