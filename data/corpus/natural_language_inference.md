# Natural Language Inference (NLI)

Natural Language Inference is the task of determining the logical relationship between two pieces of text: a premise and a hypothesis. Given a premise, a model classifies the hypothesis into one of three categories — entailment, meaning the premise logically supports or implies the hypothesis; contradiction, meaning the premise and hypothesis cannot both be true; or neutral, meaning the premise neither supports nor contradicts the hypothesis. NLI began as a standalone benchmark task in NLP research, with datasets such as SNLI and MultiNLI, but the same entailment classifiers have since become a core building block of hallucination detection.

The connection is direct: if a generated sentence is treated as the hypothesis and a retrieved evidence chunk is treated as the premise, then checking whether the evidence entails the generated sentence is exactly the question a hallucination detector needs answered. A high entailment score means the retrieved document genuinely supports the claim; a contradiction score means the generated sentence conflicts with the evidence (a particularly serious failure, since the model has effectively invented the opposite of what its own sources say); and a neutral or low-entailment score means the sentence is going beyond — or drifting away from — what the evidence actually states, the hallmark of an unsupported or fabricated claim.

Cross-encoder NLI models, such as the DeBERTa-based cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-small model, process the premise and hypothesis together in a single forward pass through the transformer, which lets the model's attention layers directly compare words and phrases across the two texts. This gives cross-encoders noticeably higher accuracy than comparing separately computed sentence embeddings for this task, at the cost of being slower, since every premise-hypothesis pair must be run through the model together rather than reusing pre-computed vectors. For hallucination detection, where each generated sentence is checked against only a handful of retrieved chunks rather than an entire corpus, this extra cost per comparison is manageable, which is why cross-encoder NLI models are a practical and popular choice for this specific verification step even in otherwise latency-conscious pipelines.

A practical design decision in NLI-based hallucination detection is which of the three output probabilities to use as the "support" signal. Most systems take the entailment probability directly, sometimes subtracting the contradiction probability, so that a sentence that is actively contradicted by evidence scores even lower than one that is merely unaddressed by it — a distinction that matters because contradicted claims are typically the most damaging kind of hallucination to let through unflagged.
