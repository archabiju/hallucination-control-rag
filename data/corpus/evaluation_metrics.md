# Evaluation Metrics for Hallucination Detection Systems

Evaluating a hallucination-detection system means measuring how well it distinguishes supported claims from unsupported ones, using a labeled test set where the correct verdict for each claim is already known. The standard metrics for this kind of binary classification problem — precision, recall, F1, and AUROC — come from the broader machine learning literature but need some care in how they are applied here, because the two possible error types have very different real-world consequences.

Precision measures, out of everything the system flagged as hallucinated, what fraction actually was hallucinated. Recall (also called sensitivity) measures, out of everything that was actually hallucinated, what fraction the system caught. These two usually trade off against each other: a system can catch more hallucinations (higher recall) by flagging more aggressively, but that typically means also flagging some genuinely correct claims as hallucinated (lower precision), and vice versa. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, giving a single number that only looks good when both are reasonably high, which is why it's commonly reported as the headline metric even though precision and recall individually tell a more complete story.

AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) evaluates a system across every possible decision threshold at once, rather than at the one threshold that was actually chosen. It plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate as the threshold sweeps from its most permissive to its most conservative setting, and the area under that curve summarizes how well the underlying support scores separate hallucinated claims from supported ones in general — a score of 1.0 means the two classes are perfectly separable by the score alone, while 0.5 means the score is no better than a random guess. AUROC is particularly useful during development, before a final threshold has been locked in, since it evaluates the quality of the scoring itself independently of any specific cutoff.

For a hallucination-detection system specifically, false negatives (a hallucinated claim that slips through as "supported") are generally considered more costly than false positives (a genuinely correct claim that gets unnecessarily flagged or blocked), because a missed hallucination can directly mislead a user who has no reason to distrust it, whereas an over-cautious block merely costs the user a moment of friction and a chance to consult the underlying evidence directly. This asymmetry is why many systems intentionally bias their threshold toward higher recall, accepting a larger number of occasionally-unnecessary flags in exchange for catching a larger share of actual hallucinations, and why reporting only accuracy (correct-verdict rate) without breaking it down by precision and recall can be misleading — a system could achieve seemingly high accuracy on an imbalanced test set simply by rarely flagging anything, while still missing most of the hallucinations that matter.
