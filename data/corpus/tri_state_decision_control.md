# Tri-State Decision Control for Generated Answers

A decision controller is the component of a hallucination-aware pipeline that takes the per-sentence support scores produced by the detection layer and turns them into a final action: show the answer, show it with caveats, or withhold it. Rather than a simple binary pass/fail, many systems use a tri-state scheme with two thresholds, producing three possible outcomes for each generated sentence or for the response as a whole.

In a typical tri-state scheme, a support score at or above a high threshold (for example 0.75) is classified as fully Supported, meaning the evidence strongly backs the claim, and the sentence is shown along with its citation. A score between a medium threshold (for example 0.50) and the high threshold is classified as Weakly Supported: the evidence partially backs the claim, or backs a related but not identical claim, so the sentence is still shown but flagged with a warning so the reader knows to treat it with more caution. A score below the medium threshold is classified as Hallucinated: the evidence does not meaningfully support the claim, and the sentence is either removed from the shown answer or the entire response is withheld, depending on how severe the failure is.

The decision to block an entire response, rather than just removing the unsupported sentence and showing the rest, typically depends on a ratio: if more than some fraction of a response's sentences (a common choice is fifty percent) are classified as Hallucinated, the system concludes that the response as a whole is unreliable — even the "supported" sentences that remain may have been generated in a context where the model was already drifting from the evidence — and suppresses the entire answer rather than presenting a partial one that could still mislead. This is a deliberately conservative design choice: a system that always shows whatever fraction of an answer happens to check out can still cause harm if the surviving fragments, taken together, paint a misleading picture, whereas a system that withholds a low-confidence answer entirely and instead surfaces the raw retrieved evidence lets the user judge the source material directly.

The two thresholds themselves are tunable parameters, not fixed constants, and the right values depend on the application's tolerance for false blocks versus false passes. Raising the high threshold makes the system more conservative — fewer answers qualify as fully supported, reducing the chance that a subtly unsupported claim slips through as "Supported," at the cost of flagging more genuinely correct answers as only weakly supported. Lowering the medium threshold has the opposite effect: fewer answers get blocked outright, at the cost of letting weaker evidence still result in a shown (if flagged) answer. Because this trade-off has no universally correct setting, a well-built system exposes both thresholds as adjustable parameters rather than hard-coding them, so the same detection scores can be re-classified under a stricter or looser policy without re-running any of the underlying models.
